Houston Water Damage Insurance Claim Guide (2026)

Most Houston water damage claims are decided by two things: where the water came from, and how well the loss was documented in the first 48 hours. This guide covers both, using the coverage rules that actually apply to Texas homeowners policies in 2026.

It is written for homeowners in Jersey Village, Houston and the surrounding suburbs who are dealing with a live loss and need to understand the process while it is happening. It is general information rather than legal or insurance advice, and your own policy language always governs.

What Standard Homeowners Policies Cover

The organising principle is sudden and accidental. Water damage that happens quickly and unexpectedly, from a source inside the home, is generally covered:

  • Burst or ruptured supply lines, including under-slab plumbing failures
  • Water heater failures and tank ruptures
  • Appliance overflows from washing machines, dishwashers and refrigerator lines
  • Frozen pipe bursts, which do occur in Houston during hard freezes
  • Rain entering through an opening created by a covered peril, such as wind removing part of the roof
  • Water used to extinguish a fire, and the damage caused by extinguishing it

Emergency mitigation, meaning extraction and drying performed to stop the damage worsening, is normally paid as part of the covered claim rather than treated as a separate expense.

What Is Excluded, and Why It Catches People Out

Rising floodwater. Water that arrives across the ground from outside is excluded, full stop, regardless of what caused it. In a metro that floods as regularly as Houston does, this is the single largest gap between what homeowners expect and what they hold. Flood coverage is a separate policy through the NFIP or a private carrier, and new policies normally carry a waiting period, so it cannot be bought once a storm is forecast.

Gradual leaks and seepage. A pipe that has dripped inside a wall for months is treated as a maintenance issue rather than an accident. Carriers will often pay to access and repair the failed component while declining the resulting damage.

Deferred maintenance. Damage from a roof already past its service life, failed caulking or a known unrepaired problem is generally denied on the grounds that the loss was foreseeable.

Sewer and drain backup, unless you carry the specific endorsement. This is inexpensive to add and frequently absent from policies until a homeowner needs it. If you are dealing with a backup now, our sewage cleanup service covers the decontamination side of the work.

Mold beyond the sub-limit. Covered when it results from a covered water event, but capped, and excluded entirely when traced to humidity or an unrepaired leak.

The First 48 Hours: What to Do in Order

  • Stop the source and make the property safe. Shut off the water at the main if the leak is internal. Cut power to affected areas at the breaker rather than reaching for switches in a wet room.
  • Document before you touch anything. Photograph and video every affected room, wide shots and close-ups, including standing water depth, wet materials and damaged contents. This is the evidence that carries the most weight later, and it cannot be recreated.
  • Report the loss to your carrier. Get a claim number, note the date and time, and record who you spoke to.
  • Begin emergency mitigation. Your policy requires reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Call a restoration contractor and start extraction immediately rather than waiting for the adjuster.
  • Keep everything. Retain damaged materials where practical, keep every receipt including hotel and meal costs if the home is uninhabitable, and keep a written log of every conversation with the carrier.

What Good Documentation Looks Like

Adjusters approve what is evidenced. The strongest claim files share the same components: dated photographs from before mitigation began, moisture readings recorded against dry-standard baselines elsewhere in the home, a daily drying log showing equipment placed and how readings moved, an itemised scope of affected materials with the reasoning for removal versus drying, and receipts for emergency work.

That last point matters more than it appears. When a contractor can show that a section of drywall was removed because the material read wet at a specific level rather than because it looked bad, scope disputes largely disappear. Our structural drying service produces that log as a standard part of every job, and it is handed to you at completion whether or not the carrier asks for it.

Mistakes That Reduce Payouts

Waiting for the adjuster before mitigating. The most expensive mistake available. Additional damage that accrued while you waited can be excluded as a failure to mitigate.

Throwing everything away immediately. The opposite error. Discarding damaged contents before they are documented removes the proof they existed.

Describing the cause loosely. Saying the house flooded when a supply line burst invites the flood exclusion into a claim that was never a flood. Describe what actually happened, accurately and specifically.

Accepting the first estimate without reading the scope. Check that the estimate includes everything found wet, not just what was visible from the doorway. Water inside wall cavities and under flooring is routinely missed in a quick walkthrough.

Letting drying stop early. If equipment comes out before verified readings, the resulting mold or floor failure often surfaces weeks later, at which point tying it back to the original claim becomes an argument.

If Your Claim Is Underpaid or Denied

Request the denial in writing with the specific policy language relied on. Compare that language with your actual policy, including endorsements, since carriers occasionally cite provisions that do not apply to your form. If the dispute is over scope rather than coverage, an independent moisture and damage assessment frequently resolves it, because the disagreement is usually about what was wet rather than what the policy says.

If the dispute persists, Texas homeowners can file a complaint with the Texas Department of Insurance, and policies typically include an appraisal clause for valuation disputes. Public adjusters and attorneys are available for larger claims, though their fees come out of the settlement, so they suit significant losses more than routine ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does homeowners insurance cover water damage in Houston?

Standard homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water damage from inside the home, such as a burst supply line, a failed water heater or an overflowing appliance. They exclude rising floodwater from outside, which requires separate flood insurance, and they exclude damage from long-term seepage or deferred maintenance. The distinction that decides most Houston claims is not how much water there was, but where it came from and how suddenly it arrived.

What is the difference between water damage and flood coverage?

Insurers define a flood as water arriving from outside and at ground level: storm surge, overflowing bayous and creeks, or surface water accumulating across the property. That is excluded from homeowners policies and is covered under a separate NFIP or private flood policy. Water originating inside the building, or entering through a storm-created opening such as a roof breach, generally falls under the homeowners policy instead.

How long do I have to file a water damage claim in Texas?

Texas policies require prompt notice, and most carriers expect notification within days rather than weeks. Statutory deadlines for the carrier to acknowledge and decide are set out in the Texas Insurance Code, but the practical risk is different: the longer you wait, the easier it becomes for the carrier to argue that some of the damage resulted from your delay rather than the original event. Report the loss as soon as it is safe to do so.

Should I start cleanup before the adjuster arrives?

Yes, and your policy generally requires it. Homeowners have a duty to mitigate, meaning you must take reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Waiting days for an adjuster while a house sits wet can result in the additional damage being denied. Photograph and video everything first, keep damaged materials where practical, retain all receipts, and begin emergency mitigation immediately.

What documentation do adjusters actually want?

Photographs and video of the damage before anything is moved, the date and cause of the loss, moisture readings taken against dry-standard baselines, a daily drying log showing equipment and readings, an itemised scope of affected materials, and receipts for emergency work. A claim supported by measured data moves faster and gets reduced less often than one supported by description alone.

Can my claim be denied if mold is involved?

Mold coverage is usually limited rather than absent. Most Texas homeowners policies carry a mold sub-limit, often somewhere between $1,000 and $10,000, and pay it when the mold resulted directly from a covered water event. Mold traced to long-term humidity, an unrepaired leak or deferred maintenance is normally excluded. Documenting the water source and the timeline from the first visit is what keeps mold attached to the covered event.
